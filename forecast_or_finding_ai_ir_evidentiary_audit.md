## Abstract

Scholarship on artificial intelligence (AI) in international relations (IR) has expanded rapidly since 2015. Its claims now circulate in policy documents, textbooks, and further scholarship. This article does not summarise that literature. It audits it. We ask a single question of every claim in a defined corpus of peer-reviewed work: what warrant does the author offer for it, and does that warrant match the kind of claim being made? We distinguish declared forecasts, which state their scope conditions and present themselves as conjecture, from circulated findings, which are conjectures that travel as if they were established results. We code [VERİ: N] articles published between 2015 and 2025, disaggregated into [VERİ: n] claims, by claim type, warrant type, and the author's own epistemological positioning. We assess fit between claim and warrant relative to that positioning, not relative to a single positivist standard. We then weight claims by citation count to test whether the most cited claims are also the best warranted. Findings are reported at the aggregate level. The article closes with research design specifications for testing the three most central claims in the corpus. The contribution is a coding instrument, an aggregate map of the field's evidentiary base, and a set of testable designs.

**Keywords:** artificial intelligence, international security, systematic review, evidentiary standards, research design, meta-research

## 1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence entered the IR literature as a subject of forecasting. Early work asked what AI would do to the balance of power, to deterrence, to the speed of crisis decision-making, and to the diffusion of military capability (Horowitz 2018; Payne 2018; Johnson 2019). Forecasting was the appropriate mode. The technology was immature, deployment was limited, and observable cases were scarce.

A decade later the field has changed in size. Whether it has changed in mode is less clear. Claims made in 2017 and 2018 continue to be cited, and our working hypothesis is that some of them are now cited as premises rather than as hypotheses. That is a conjecture about the literature, not a finding, and we state it as such. Whether it holds is an empirical question. This article is designed to answer it.

Our question is not whether the literature is good or bad. It is narrower. For each claim in a defined corpus, we ask: what does the author offer as grounds, and is that offering adequate for the type of claim made? A conceptual claim needs conceptual grounds. A causal claim needs evidence capable of bearing on causation. A forecast needs stated scope conditions and an account of what would falsify it. Mismatch between claim type and warrant type is the object of the audit. Absence of evidence is not, by itself, a defect. A well-flagged conjecture is a legitimate scholarly product.

The distinction that organises the article is between a **declared forecast** and a **circulated finding**. A declared forecast states its scope conditions, presents itself as conjecture, and identifies the observation that would count against it. A circulated finding is a conjecture that appears in later work without those markers, as a settled result. The two can have identical propositional content. What differs is their epistemic status and the way that status is signalled. We hold that the field's problem, if it has one, lies in the transition from the first to the second, not in the existence of the first. Section 3 develops this distinction. It is the conceptual contribution of the article.

Three features of the design distinguish this audit from a literature review. First, the unit of analysis is the claim, not the article. A single article routinely contains claims of different types with different warrants. Scoring the article as a whole would collapse the audit back into summary. Second, every article is coded first for its own epistemological positioning and only then for internal consistency. Interpretivist and critical work is assessed against interpretivist and critical standards. Positivist work is assessed against positivist standards. The audit measures whether authors meet the standards they set for themselves. Third, findings are weighted by citation count. This allows a test of whether the field's reception mechanisms favour well-warranted claims or poorly warranted ones.

We are aware that an audit of evidentiary standards must meet its own standards. Every empirical claim in this article rests on our own coded data or is explicitly marked as conjecture. Where we lack data, we say so. Where our reliability statistics are weak, we report them. The protocol was fixed before coding began and is reproduced in full in the appendices.

The article proceeds as follows. Section 2 situates the audit in three bodies of work: the AI–IR literature organised by claim family, meta-research on evidentiary standards in IR, and research on citation dynamics. Section 3 sets out the forecast/finding distinction and its relationship to existing work on argument structure and expert prediction. Section 4 presents the coding scheme: claim types, warrant types, positioning codes, and the fit matrix that links them. Section 5 describes the search protocol, sampling, coding procedure, and reliability. Section 6 reports aggregate findings. Section 7 specifies research designs for testing the three most central claims. Section 8 states limitations. Section 9 concludes with a research agenda.

## 2. Three Literatures

The audit draws on three bodies of work. The first is the object of the audit: peer-reviewed scholarship on AI in IR and security studies. The second is meta-research on evidentiary standards within IR itself. The third is research from other disciplines on how claims acquire authority through citation. This section describes each and states what the audit takes from it. It does not summarise findings; it identifies claim families and warrant conventions that the coding scheme in Section 4 must be able to capture.

### 2.1 The AI–IR literature by claim family

Pilot reading of the corpus suggests that the substantive literature organises around six recurring claim families. We state this as a pre-coding observation, not as a finding; the coded distribution appears in Section 6. The families are listed here because the coding scheme must be able to classify each of them, and because the candidate claims in Section 7 are drawn from them.

The same hedge covers the warrant characterisations in this section. Each family description below ends with an impression of the kind of grounds its authors typically offer. Those impressions come from reading, not from coding, and several of them are exactly the propositions this article exists to test. Every one of them may be overturned by Section 6.3, and none should be read as a result. We phrase them in the present indicative for readability and mark them here, once, as conjecture rather than repeating the qualifier six times.

**Diffusion and adoption.** The oldest family in the corpus applies established theories of military innovation to AI. The framework of adoption capacity, developed for earlier technologies (Horowitz 2010), is extended to AI in the argument that organisational and financial barriers, not access to algorithms, will determine which states field military AI first (Horowitz 2018; Horowitz 2020). Related work on imitation and reverse engineering in complex weapons systems provides the comparative baseline (Gilli and Gilli 2019). More recent work reframes diffusion in terms of general-purpose technologies and the institutions that spread engineering skills across an economy (Ding 2024). The typical warrant in this family is historical analogy and deduction from an existing framework, with case illustration.

**Strategic stability and escalation.** A second family asks whether AI-enabled sensing, targeting, and decision support destabilise deterrence. The counterforce argument that improvements in accuracy and remote sensing threaten survivable second-strike forces (Lieber and Press 2017) is the anchor for claims about AI and nuclear stability. The special issue edited by Sechser, Narang, and Talmadge (2019) set out to test emerging-technology claims against historical data and reported that effects were conditional and sometimes contradictory. Within it, the Cold War evidence on intra-war escalation cast doubt on technology as an independent driver of escalation (Talmadge 2019). Work on the capability/vulnerability paradox links dependence on networked systems to first-strike incentives (Schneider 2019). Claims about delegation of strategic decisions to machines and the risk of automated escalation form a sub-family (Johnson 2022). Warrants here range from historical case analysis to deduction; this family is where the transition from declared forecast to circulated finding is most consequential, because its claims travel into nuclear policy debates.

**Crisis decision-making and human–machine interaction.** A third family, more recent and more experimental, examines how decision-makers respond to AI-generated advice. Survey experiments on crisis decisions with algorithmic input (Horowitz and Lin-Greenberg 2022) and wargame evidence on remote systems and escalation control (Lin-Greenberg 2022) supply original data. Work on familiarity and trust in military AI (Horowitz et al. 2023) and on the division of labour between machine prediction and human judgment (Goldfarb and Lindsay 2022) belongs here. This is the one family in which original evidence, W1 and W2 in the terms of Section 4, is prominent, though it is not uniform: the experimental work supplies original data, while the argument about prediction and judgment is conceptual and deductive. This family is the natural site for testing claims that elsewhere circulate on analogy alone, which is why two of the three designs in Section 7 build on it.

**Alliances and multinational operations.** A fourth, smaller family asks how AI affects burden-sharing, interoperability, and coalition decision-making (Lin-Greenberg 2020). Warrants are deductive with case illustration.

**Norms, agency, and critique.** A fifth family works from interpretivist or critical positions. It examines how autonomous weapons systems reshape norms of appropriate use through practice rather than through deliberate regulation (Bode and Huelss 2018), how technology has been conceptualised in IR theory (Fritsch 2011; McCarthy 2013), and how algorithmic systems reconfigure ethical and political agency (Amoore 2020). This family also carries the literature on regulation and arms control for lethal autonomous systems (Garcia 2018). The warrants are documentary, discursive, and conceptual. Under the positioning rule of Section 4.3, these articles are assessed against their own standards, and the coding scheme must be able to distinguish a constitutive claim from a covering-law causal claim.

**Balance of power and geoeconomics.** A sixth family treats AI as a strategic asset and asks under what conditions states will compete over it (Ding and Dafoe 2021) and how technological change interacts with international order more broadly (Drezner 2019; Jensen, Whyte, and Cuomo 2020). Warrants are conceptual and deductive with historical illustration.

Two further observations from pilot reading shape the design. First, several foundational texts are books or reports rather than journal articles (Scharre 2018; Payne 2021; Johnson 2021). The corpus excludes them, but it captures how journal articles cite them; the W3 sub-code in Section 4.2 is designed for that purpose. Second, the literature on AI has a close precedent in the literature on cyber conflict, where early forecasts of a cyber revolution (Kello 2013) were followed by empirically grounded scepticism (Gartzke 2013; Lindsay 2013). That sequence is a model of a field correcting its forecasts. Whether the AI literature has followed the same sequence is an empirical question the audit is designed to answer, and we do not prejudge it.

### 2.2 Evidentiary standards in IR

IR has a sustained internal debate about what counts as adequate grounds for a claim. Three strands matter here.

The first is philosophical. Jackson (2011) classifies methodological positions as distinct wagers about the relationship between knowledge and the world, and argues that evaluating one position by another's criteria is incoherent. The audit adopts this stance directly: it is the basis for the positioning rule and for the decision to measure fit relative to the author's own standard rather than to a single standard.

The second is practical. Debates about simplistic hypothesis testing (Mearsheimer and Walt 2013), about the costs of paradigm-based theorising (Lake 2011), and about the drift of graduate training toward particular methods (Colgan 2016) all concern the match between claim and warrant, though none uses those terms. Work on process tracing supplies explicit standards for what qualitative evidence must show to support a causal inference (Bennett and Checkel 2015), and general treatments of social science methodology supply the vocabulary of descriptive and causal inference used in Section 4 (Gerring 2012). The audit's warrant categories are drawn from this material.

The third strand is the older literature on technology and military power, which supplies a track record. Claims about the offense–defense balance of technology were made for decades before being tested against systematic evidence (Levy 1984; Lieber 2005; Biddle 2004). That literature shows both that untested technological claims can persist for a long time and that they can, eventually, be tested. The audit treats it as the closest precedent for what the AI literature might become.

IR has no established tradition of systematic review comparable to medicine or psychology. We are not aware of a claim-level, protocol-registered review in any of the field's main journals; if one exists, it is a precedent we would welcome and have missed. The PRISMA framework (Page et al. 2021) was developed for clinical evidence and requires adaptation for a literature in which the unit is a claim and the warrant is often non-statistical. Section 5 describes that adaptation.

### 2.3 How claims acquire authority

The forecast/finding distinction in Section 3 rests on a mechanism: qualifiers are lost as claims are cited. Research from outside IR documents this mechanism directly.

The closest conceptual antecedent is Latour's (1987) account of modalities. A statement, on his analysis, is accompanied by clauses that point either back toward the conditions under which it was produced or forward, away from them. Strip the backward-pointing clauses across a chain of citations and the statement ceases to be someone's contested proposal and becomes a fact that everyone knows. Our declared forecast is a statement carrying its modalities; our circulated finding is the same statement after they have been removed. We claim no novelty for the mechanism. What we add is a measurement instrument: Latour's account is ethnographic and demonstrated on cases, and it does not yield a rate. The three indicators of Section 4.5 are an attempt to make the presence or absence of modalities countable across a corpus, and to do so in a field with plural epistemologies, where what counts as stripping a qualifier cannot be judged against a single standard. Relatedly, Merton's (1968) Matthew effect supplies the reason to expect that the process is not neutral with respect to which claims travel: citation advantage accrues to the already cited, independently of the grounds on offer. That is the expectation Section 6.6 tests.

Greenberg (2009) constructed the complete citation network for a single biomedical claim and showed how unfounded authority was built through three distortions: citation bias against papers that weakened the claim, amplification by papers that cited it without adding data, and invention, including the conversion of a hypothesis into a fact in later citing texts. The third distortion is the forecast-to-finding transition described in this article. Simulation work shows that publication bias alone can canonise false claims when negative results go unpublished (Nissen et al. 2016), and the broader argument about the fragility of published findings under low prior probability and analytic flexibility (Ioannidis 2005) applies to any field whose foundational claims are rarely tested.

On forecasting specifically, the finding that expert political forecasts are often no better than simple base rates, and that experts rarely revise after failure (Tetlock 2005), motivates the declared-forecast indicators in Section 4.5. The remedy proposed in later work is procedural: forecasts should be stated in scoreable form with time horizons (Tetlock and Gardner 2015). Within IR, the case for treating prediction as a legitimate scholarly aim has been made explicitly (Ward 2016). The audit takes from this literature the principle that a forecast which cannot be scored cannot be corrected.

The audit's contribution to these literatures is to apply the citation-distortion logic to a social science field with plural epistemologies, where "conversion of hypothesis into fact" cannot be judged against a single statistical standard and must instead be judged against the author's own positioning.

## 3. Declared Forecast and Circulated Finding

### 3.1 The problem of status

Any claim about a new technology carries an implicit claim about its own status. "AI will compress decision time in crises" can be offered as a definition, a deduction from stated premises, an inference from analogous cases, a summary of observed deployments, or a guess. The sentence looks the same in each case. The reader's ability to weigh it depends on knowing which it is.

Toulmin (1958) separated the claim from its grounds, its warrant, its qualifier, and its rebuttal. The qualifier states how strongly the claim is held. The rebuttal states the conditions under which the warrant would not apply. Our three indicators in Section 4.5 map onto this structure: scope conditions and conjectural status are qualifiers, and the falsifier is a rebuttal. In mature empirical literatures, qualifiers are conventionalised through statistical reporting. In literatures about emerging phenomena, qualifiers must be stated in prose, and they are easy to omit. Omission is not dishonesty. It is often a stylistic choice made under word limits. But its aggregate effect over a decade of citation is that qualifiers disappear and claims harden.

Tetlock (2005) showed that expert forecasts in political domains are frequently no better than simple base rates and that experts rarely update after failure. His remedy was procedural: forecasts should be stated in falsifiable form, with time horizons, so they can be scored. We adopt the same logic for a scholarly literature. A forecast that cannot be scored cannot be corrected. A field that cannot correct its forecasts will accumulate them.

### 3.2 Two ideal types

We define the two ideal types as follows.

A **declared forecast** has three properties. It states scope conditions: the circumstances under which the claim is expected to hold. It signals its own status as conjecture through explicit language or through the structure of the argument. And it identifies, at least in outline, what observation would count against it.

A **circulated finding** is a claim of the same propositional content that appears without those three properties. In the originating text, this may be a matter of omission. In citing texts, it is typically a matter of transmission: the citing author reports the claim and not its qualifier. Neither is a category of blame. The audit codes the property, not the intent.

Between the ideal types sits a continuum. A claim may state scope conditions but not falsifiers. It may be flagged as conjecture in one paragraph and asserted flatly in the abstract. Our coding scheme captures these intermediate cases through three binary indicators rather than one composite score. Section 4 details them.

### 3.3 Why the distinction matters for this field

Two features of the AI–IR literature make the distinction consequential.

First, the literature is young and its foundational claims are recent. In older literatures, a claim from 1985 has had forty years of testing before it is cited as established. In this literature, a claim from 2018 may be cited as established by 2021. The interval available for correction is short.

Second, the literature has a direct policy audience. Claims about AI and strategic stability appear in official strategy documents; the final report of the US National Security Commission on Artificial Intelligence, for example, states that AI will change the speed of conflict and the character of deterrence (NSCAI 2021). When a scholarly conjecture is transmitted to a policy text, the qualifier is even less likely to survive. This is a hypothesis about transmission, not a finding of this article, and we do not test it here. We state it because it motivates the audit.

We do not claim that circulated findings are more common in this literature than in others. We have no comparative data and make no comparative claim. We claim only that the distinction is measurable and that measuring it is useful.

## 4. Coding Scheme

The coding scheme has four components: claim type, warrant type, epistemological positioning, and fit. Each is coded at the claim level. The full codebook with decision rules and examples is in Appendix A.

### 4.1 Claim types

We identify five claim types, plus a residual sixth code, "other", for claims that fit none of the five. The six values together are exhaustive. Each claim carries exactly one claim type, so that the distribution in Section 6.2 has one row per claim and its shares sum to one. The frequency of "other" is reported, and if it exceeds five percent of claims the scheme is revised under the change-log procedure in Section 5.1.

**Descriptive claims** assert that something is the case about the world: a state has deployed a system, a capability exists, an actor has adopted a doctrine. Example form: "Country X has integrated machine learning into its intelligence analysis pipeline."

**Conceptual claims** propose or defend a definition, typology, or framework. Example form: "AI is best understood as a general-purpose technology rather than as a weapon."

**Causal claims** assert that one thing produces, increases, decreases, or conditions another. Example form: "AI-enabled targeting reduces the survivability of mobile missile launchers."

**Predictive claims** assert what will happen, or what would happen under specified conditions, in the future. Example form: "States with weak civil–military integration will adopt military AI more slowly."

**Normative claims** assert what ought to be done. Example form: "States should agree to a moratorium on autonomous nuclear launch."

Where a sentence contains more than one claim, it is split into separate claims, each with its own record.

A single claim can still carry two types at once. "Because AI compresses decision time, escalation risk will rise" is one proposition asserting both a causal mechanism and a future consequence; splitting it would misrepresent the author, who advances it as one argument. For these cases the coder records the type that carries the author's assertion as the **claim type** and the other as a **secondary claim type**. Where a claim is both causal and predictive, the predictive type is primary, because it is the temporal reach of the claim that determines which evidentiary standard applies and which declared-forecast indicators are required.

Distributions in Section 6.2 are computed on the primary type only, so that each claim is counted once. The share of claims carrying a secondary type is reported separately, and fit is assessed against the primary type. This is a departure from coding both types symmetrically, which would make the shares in Section 6.2 sum to more than one and would leave the fit matrix without a single row to look up.

### 4.2 Warrant types

We identify eight warrant types. A claim may carry more than one warrant. Coders record all warrants present and identify the primary one.

| Code | Warrant type | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| W1 | Original quantitative evidence | Data collected or assembled by the author, analysed statistically |
| W2 | Original qualitative evidence | Case study, interviews, archival work, or process tracing conducted by the author |
| W3 | Secondary empirical evidence | Citation of another author's data or findings as grounds |
| W4 | Historical analogy | Inference from a prior technology or episode presented as comparable |
| W5 | Deductive or formal reasoning | Derivation from stated premises, with or without a formal model |
| W6 | Authority or expert judgment | Citation of an official, practitioner, or expert assertion without underlying data |
| W7 | Declared conjecture | The author explicitly marks the claim as speculative or hypothetical |
| W8 | No stated warrant | The claim is asserted without grounds of any of the above types |

W3 requires a further sub-code: whether the cited source itself carries W1 or W2, or whether it carries W4 through W8. Tracing stops at one step. A cited source that is itself W3 is sub-coded as W3, and the chain is not followed further within this audit; the full-network extension is set out in Section 9. One step is enough to distinguish a claim grounded in someone else's data from a claim grounded in someone else's conjecture, which is the distinction the audit needs.

### 4.3 Epistemological positioning

Each article, not each claim, is coded for the author's stated or evident epistemological position. We use four codes, following Jackson (2011) in treating them as distinct wagers about what counts as knowledge rather than as a hierarchy.

| Code | Positioning | Indicator |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Neo-positivist | Seeks generalisable causal claims; treats evidence as adjudicating between hypotheses |
| P2 | Interpretivist | Seeks understanding of meaning and practice; treats evidence as illustrating or reconstructing |
| P3 | Critical | Seeks to expose structures of power or assumptions; treats evidence as exemplifying |
| P4 | Theory-building or policy-analytic | Seeks to propose frameworks or options; treats evidence as motivating |

Positioning is coded from the author's own methodological statements where present. Where absent, coders infer positioning from the structure of the argument and record that the inference was made.

It is worth being exact about where positioning does its work, because it is easy to overstate. Positioning changes a fit value at exactly one point in the matrix: a predictive claim resting on a developed analogy is adequate under P2, P3, and P4 and partial under P1, because framework-building and interpretive work treat a sustained analogy as an argument in itself while neo-positivist work treats it as a substitute for evidence. Everywhere else the matrix is the same under all four codes.

Positioning does most of its work earlier, in classification rather than in scoring. Whether a passage in a critical article asserts a causal claim at all, whether "produces" is meant in a covering-law or a constitutive sense, whether a documented discourse counts as original qualitative evidence: these are decided by the author's own standard, and they determine which row and column the claim lands in. A P3 article claiming that a discourse produces a security logic is coded as a causal claim warranted by W2, and the matrix then returns adequate. Applying positivist evidentiary standards at that upstream step would be a category error, and the audit would fail its own test.

We make this explicit rather than resting on the claim that the design is positioning-relative throughout. It is positioning-relative in classification and at one scoring rule, and a reader is entitled to know which.

### 4.4 Fit

Fit is a judgment about whether the warrant type is adequate to the claim type, given the article's positioning. The fit matrix in Appendix A states, for each combination, whether the warrant is **adequate**, **partial**, or **inadequate**. Some general rules:

The rules below restate the matrix in prose. Where prose and matrix appear to differ, the matrix governs, and the discrepancy is a defect to be logged and corrected rather than a coder's choice.

- A conceptual claim is adequately warranted by W1 through W5 or by W7, and partially by W6 or W8. Conceptual work is the one place where declared conjecture is a sufficient warrant on its own.
- A causal claim is adequately warranted by W1, by W2, or by W3 whose sub-code shows the cited source carries W1 or W2. W3 tracing anywhere else, W4, W5, and W7 are partial. W6 and W8 are inadequate.
- A predictive claim is adequately warranted by W1, by W2, by W3 tracing to W1 or W2, or by W7 carrying all three declared-forecast indicators. W7 without all three is partial, as are W3 tracing elsewhere, W4, W5, and W6. W8 is inadequate.
- A descriptive claim is inadequately warranted by W5, because a claim about what is the case is not established by deduction, and by W8.
- Under P2 and P3, causal language is often used in a constitutive rather than a covering-law sense. Coders apply the author's own standard when deciding whether a passage asserts a causal claim at all, and when identifying the warrant offered. A P3 article claiming that a discourse "produces" a security logic is adequately warranted by W2 if the discourse is documented, which is what the causal row already gives.
- Under P2, P3, and P4, a predictive claim warranted by a developed analogy is adequate; under P1 it is partial. This is the single fit value that positioning changes.
- Causal claims in P4 articles are coded for fit on the same rules and additionally flagged, and reported separately.
- Normative claims are coded on the warrant offered for their empirical premise, using the Normative row of the matrix. The normative inference itself is not assessed.

Fit is coded conservatively. Where coders disagree after discussion, the claim is coded as partial and the disagreement is logged. We note the consequence of that rule in Section 8: it moves disagreement into the middle category, so the partial share absorbs coder uncertainty and the adequate and inadequate shares are, to that extent, conservative estimates.

### 4.5 Declared-forecast indicators

For every predictive claim and every causal claim about future effects, coders record three binary indicators:

1. **Scope conditions stated.** The text specifies the circumstances under which the claim is expected to hold.
2. **Conjectural status signalled.** The text uses explicit language ("we expect," "it is plausible that," "hypothesis") or structural markers (a hypotheses section, a "future research" framing).
3. **Falsifier identified.** The text states, in any form, what observation would count against the claim.

A claim with all three indicators is a declared forecast. A claim with none is a circulated finding. Intermediate cases are recorded as such, as three separate binary fields rather than a composite score, because the pilot suggests the three do not move together.

Measuring transmission requires two readings of the same proposition, and the coding records both. The claim as it appears in the article being coded is the **citing text**, and its three indicators are the fields already described. Where that claim is warranted by W3, the coder retrieves the cited source, locates the proposition as the original author stated it, and records the same three indicators a second time for that **originating text**. The pair is what Table 4 compares; a claim contributes to Table 4 only if both readings exist. Where the cited source cannot be retrieved, the originating indicators are recorded as "unknown" and the claim is excluded from the paired comparison, with the number excluded reported alongside the table.

This is the measurement on which RQ3 and H2 depend, and it is the most labour-intensive part of the coding, since it requires opening every cited source behind a W3 claim rather than reading only the article in hand.

### 4.6 Worked examples

Two examples show the scheme in use. The first is drawn from the corpus; the protocol permits identifying real articles only as positive examples. The second is constructed.

*Example 1.* Horowitz and Lin-Greenberg (2022) report survey experiments on how algorithmic advice affects crisis decisions. A representative claim is that the source of a recommendation, human or machine, conditions how decision-makers weigh it. Coding: claim type causal; primary warrant W1; positioning P1; fit adequate. The article states the experimental conditions under which the result holds, which satisfies the scope-condition indicator, and the hypotheses are stated before the results, which satisfies the conjectural-status indicator for the pre-test formulation.

*Example 2 (constructed).* An article positioned as P1 asserts in its introduction that "AI-enabled early warning will shorten the window for human deliberation in nuclear crises," cites two earlier articles, and offers no data of its own. Retrieval shows both cited articles ground the claim in analogy to Cold War early-warning systems. Coding: claim type predictive, secondary claim type causal, since the proposition asserts both a mechanism and a future consequence and the predictive reach governs; primary warrant W3, sub-coded as tracing to W4; positioning P1; fit partial. Declared-forecast indicators: scope conditions absent, conjectural status absent, falsifier absent. The originating claim in the cited articles may have carried all three indicators; if so, the loss is recorded in Table 4.

The second example is the pattern the audit is built to detect. It is not an accusation against any article; it is a description of a coding outcome that the scheme must be able to produce reliably.

### 5.2 Search

We searched Web of Science Core Collection and Scopus for peer-reviewed articles published between 1 January 2015 and 31 December 2025. The search string combined AI terms with IR and security terms in title, abstract, and keywords. The full string is in Appendix C. The 2015 start date was chosen on the basis of annual hit counts for the search string, reported in Appendix C; [VERİ: yıllık sayılar] show the point at which AI terms begin to appear in the indexed IR and security literature at more than incidental frequency. Earlier work framed as "automation" or "robotics" is out of scope, and this boundary is a limitation noted in Section 8.

Records were deduplicated across databases. Titles and abstracts were screened by two coders independently against the inclusion criteria. Full texts of records passing screening were retrieved and screened again. The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Page et al. 2021) is in Appendix B.

### 5.3 Inclusion and exclusion

Included: peer-reviewed research articles in journals indexed in the IR, political science, or security studies categories of either database; substantive engagement with AI as a variable, concept, or object of analysis; English language.

Excluded: book reviews, editorials, and forum contributions without original argument; articles in which AI appears only as an illustrative example; articles in computer science or law venues without an IR or security framing; grey literature; books and book chapters.

The exclusion of grey literature and books is a limitation, discussed in Section 8. It is also a design choice. Peer review is the mechanism through which the field certifies claims. Auditing peer-reviewed work audits that mechanism.

### 5.5 Claim extraction

For each included article, coders extracted every claim appearing in the abstract, the introduction, and the conclusion, and every claim in the body text that the author marks as a contribution, hypothesis, or finding. Claims in literature review sections that report other authors' positions without endorsement were not extracted. The extraction rule was tested on a pilot of [VERİ: n] articles and revised once before the protocol was frozen; the revision is documented in Appendix D.

### 5.6 Coding and reliability

Two coders with graduate training in IR coded independently. Coders were not blind to author identity; full blinding is impossible in a small field, and we note this in Section 8. [VERİ: %] of the sample, selected at random, was double coded. Reliability was assessed at two stages. First, unitisation: because coders extract claims before coding them, two coders can disagree about how many claims a passage contains and where each begins. We report Krippendorff's unitising α for the double-coded subset and reconciled claim boundaries before variable coding. Second, variable coding. We report Krippendorff's nominal α (Krippendorff 2018) for each variable: claim type, primary warrant type, positioning, fit, and each of the three declared-forecast indicators. Our pre-registered threshold was α ≥ 0.70. [VERİ: Her değişken için α değeri. Eşiğin altında kalan varsa: şema revize edildi mi, yeniden kodlama yapıldı mı, sonuç ne oldu.] Disagreements were resolved by discussion. Unresolved disagreements were coded as "partial" for fit and logged.

### 5.7 Citation weighting

Citation counts were retrieved from [VERİ: WoS / Scopus / Google Scholar] on [VERİ: tarih]. Because citation counts grow with time since publication, we compute citations per year and, separately, rank each article within its publication year. Both measures are reported. We test the association between citation rank and (a) the share of claims with adequate fit and (b) the share of predictive claims with all three declared-forecast indicators, using Spearman rank correlation with bootstrapped confidence intervals, computed within publication year and pooled. The analysis is descriptive; it does not identify a causal effect of warrant quality on citation.

Two further rules apply. First, a citation is not an endorsement. For the claim-level analysis in Section 6.7, each citing passage is coded for valence: supporting, neutral, or critical. Only supporting and neutral citations count toward a claim's centrality. Second, claims from different articles are grouped as "the same claim" only when they match an entry in a canonical claim list built during the pilot and frozen with the protocol. Coders assign each extracted claim to a canonical entry or to "none"; α for this assignment is reported with the other reliability statistics.

### 5.8 What this design cannot do

The design measures the relationship between claim and warrant within texts. It does not measure whether claims are true. A poorly warranted claim may be correct. A well-warranted claim may be wrong. The audit is about the transparency and adequacy of grounds, not about outcomes. Section 7 addresses how outcomes could be tested.

## 7. Research Designs for Testing the Central Claims

This section specifies how the three most central claims could be tested. The final selection of claims is determined by Section 6.7. Pending that, we specify designs for three candidate claims selected before coding, on the strength of their prominence in the claim families described in Section 2.1. Two of the three appeared in the two-article pilot; the third did not, and we say so in its subsection rather than implying a coded profile it does not have. The pilot covered two articles and twenty-one claims, is not protocol data, and cannot establish centrality. If the final ranking in Section 6.7 differs from these three, this section is rewritten to match. The designs are the third closing sentence of the article.

Each design states: the claim in falsifiable form, the observable implications, the data required, the identification strategy, and the observation that would count against the claim.

### 7.1 Candidate claim A: adoption capacity conditions military AI diffusion

**Claim in falsifiable form.** Holding resources constant, states with higher organisational adoption capacity (measured by prior record of integrating new military technologies and by civil–military technology transfer institutions) will field operational military AI systems earlier and in more domains than states with lower capacity.

**Origin in the corpus.** This claim extends the diffusion framework of Horowitz (2010) to AI (Horowitz 2018). It appeared twice in the two-article pilot, both times as a causal claim in a P4 article, and on both occasions its primary warrant was W3 sub-coded as tracing to original qualitative evidence. Two instances in two articles establish nothing about the corpus; the coded profile is reported in Section 6.7.

**Observable implications.** Ordering of first operational deployment across states; breadth of deployment across domains; time from public demonstration to fielding.

**Data required.** A cross-national dataset of publicly documented military AI deployments, coded by domain and by date of first operational (not experimental) use. Independent measures of adoption capacity constructed from pre-2015 records to avoid endogeneity. Candidate sources: defence procurement records, parliamentary budget documents, and verified reporting; the dataset must record source quality for each entry.

**Identification.** Event-history analysis of time to first deployment with adoption capacity as the main predictor and GDP, military expenditure, and threat environment as controls. Because deployment is partly secret, the analysis must model censoring explicitly and should be repeated on the subset of states with high reporting transparency.

**Falsifier.** A pre-registered minimum effect size for adoption capacity, net of resources and threat environment. If the estimated association falls below that threshold with a confidence interval that excludes it, the claim fails in its stated form.

### 7.2 Candidate claim B: AI compresses crisis decision time and raises escalation risk

**Claim in falsifiable form.** Decision-makers presented with AI-generated assessments in a crisis will make escalatory choices more quickly and more often than decision-makers presented with equivalent assessments from human analysts.

**Origin in the corpus.** This claim appeared twice in the two-article pilot, both times as a predictive claim in a P4 article: once with primary warrant W6, an expert assertion carrying no underlying data, and once with W4, an analogy to NATO warning time and ballistic-missile-defence pre-delegation developed over a full subsection. Neither instance identified a falsifier. Two instances establish nothing about the corpus; the coded profile is reported in Section 6.7. Experimental work has begun to test related propositions (Horowitz and Lin-Greenberg 2022; Lin-Greenberg 2022), which is why this claim is a strong candidate for a design that builds on W1.

**Observable implications.** In controlled settings, difference in decision latency and in escalation choice by source of assessment. In observational settings, difference in crisis duration and outcome where AI-enabled early warning is known to be present.

**Data required.** Survey experiments with elite and near-elite samples (serving or retired officers, defence civilians), varying the stated source of assessment and holding content constant. The design replicates the source manipulation of Horowitz and Lin-Greenberg (2022) and adds decision latency as an outcome, since the compression component of the claim concerns speed and existing experiments measure choice. Wargame data with randomised assignment of AI-assisted versus human-assisted cells.

**Identification.** Randomised assignment of assessment source. Pre-registered outcome measures: time to decision, escalation level chosen, confidence rating. Manipulation checks to confirm participants registered the source.

**Falsifier.** If participants given AI-sourced assessments do not decide faster and do not escalate more, across at least two elite samples, the claim fails. If they decide faster but do not escalate more, the compression component survives and the escalation component fails; the claim must be split.

### 7.3 Candidate claim C: AI-enabled sensing degrades the survivability of second-strike forces

**Claim in falsifiable form.** Improvements in machine-learning-based sensor fusion and target recognition since 2015 have measurably reduced the expected survivability of mobile nuclear delivery systems against a first strike.

**Origin in the corpus.** This claim builds on the counterforce argument of Lieber and Press (2017) and on the vulnerability paradox of Schneider (2019). **It did not appear in the two-article pilot**, which covered diffusion and alliance topics and reached no article in the strategic-stability family. It therefore has no pilot warrant profile, and we report none. It is included here as a candidate on the strength of the prominence of the strategic-stability family described in Section 2.1, and it is retained in this section only if Section 6.7 ranks it among the three most central claims.

**Observable implications.** Reduction in time to detect and classify mobile launchers in open-source or declassified test data; changes in force posture (dispersal, hardening, doctrinal shifts) by states whose forces are the presumed targets.

**Data required.** This is the hardest of the three because the relevant performance data is classified. Two partial routes exist. First, open benchmark performance of target recognition models on satellite imagery, tracked over time, as a lower bound on state capability. Second, posture indicators: changes in patrol patterns, investment in mobility and concealment, and doctrinal statements, coded from public sources.

**Identification.** The claim cannot be tested directly with public data. It can be tested indirectly: if states with second-strike forces have not changed posture in ways consistent with perceived degradation, either the degradation has not occurred or it has not been perceived. Interviews with former officials can distinguish these.

**Falsifier.** If open benchmark performance has not improved on the relevant task since 2015, or if target states show no posture change and former officials report no perceived change, the claim fails in its stated form. A null result on both routes counts against the claim but cannot rule out classified improvements that have produced no visible posture change; the result should be reported with that boundary stated.

### 7.4 Common requirements

All three designs require pre-registration of hypotheses and outcome measures. All three require that null results be reported. Two of the three (A and C) require the construction of public datasets that do not currently exist. Building those datasets is itself a research agenda, and it is the agenda we recommend in Section 9.

## 8. Limitations

**Language.** The corpus is English only. Chinese, Russian, and other language literatures on AI and security are excluded. Any claim about "the field" in this article means the English-language, peer-reviewed field indexed in WoS and Scopus.

**Venue.** Books, chapters, and grey literature are excluded. Some of the most cited work on AI and security is in book or report form. Our corpus captures how those works are cited in journals but does not audit them directly.

**Indexing bias.** WoS and Scopus over-represent journals based in North America and Western Europe. The corpus inherits that bias.

**Coder judgment.** Fit is a judgment. We have reported reliability and adopted a conservative rule for disagreement, but the judgment remains one that other coders could make differently. The codebook is public so that they can.

**The disagreement rule inflates the middle category.** Unresolved disagreements are coded partial. Partial is also a substantive value, assigned by the matrix to a large number of warrant and claim-type combinations. The two are not distinguished in the reported distribution, so the partial share contains both claims that are genuinely partially warranted and claims on which two coders could not agree. This makes the adequate and inadequate shares conservative, and it means the partial share should not be read as a substantive finding on its own. We report the number of claims that reached partial by the disagreement rule separately, so that a reader can subtract it.

**The canonical claim list is frozen on a small pilot.** Claims from different articles are grouped as the same claim only when they match an entry in a list built during the pilot. A list built on a small number of articles will under-represent claims that recur later in the corpus, and a claim absent from the list is coded as "none" and cannot contribute to the centrality ranking in Section 6.7. Any claim assigned "none" by more than a threshold share of coders is a candidate for addition, and additions after the freeze are logged in Appendix D. This is a known weakness of the design rather than a solved problem.

**Two rules are applied outside the matrix.** The declared-forecast test and the developed-analogy test are applied by formula rather than by lookup. Both are stated in Appendix A.5 and both are reproducible from the published workbook, but a reader reconstructing fit from the matrix alone would not recover them.

**Citation as a measure of centrality.** Citation counts are shaped by journal prestige, author networks, and time since publication, and they do not distinguish endorsement from critique. We address the last through valence coding and the others through within-year ranking, but the measure remains an imperfect proxy for a claim's influence in the field.

**Time confound.** If the field's evidentiary base has improved over time, recent articles will carry both fewer citations and better warrants. A raw negative association between citation and fit could then reflect maturation rather than reception. The within-year analysis is designed to separate these, and we report both.

**Blinding.** Coders could not be blinded to author identity. Coder expectations about particular authors or journals may have affected fit judgments in ways the reliability statistics do not fully capture.

**Truth.** The audit does not assess whether claims are correct. Section 7 describes how that could be done for three claims. It is not done here.

**Self-application.** We applied the coding scheme to this article. [VERİ: Sonuç. ## 9. Conclusion and Research Agenda

The audit produces three sentences.

First: [VERİ: X% of the field's claims rest on original or traced empirical evidence; Y% on analogy or deduction; Z% on declared conjecture or no stated warrant.]

Second: [VERİ: The most cited claims are more / less / equally well warranted than the median claim, with association of magnitude R.]

Third: the three most central claims can be tested with the designs in Section 7, two of which require datasets that do not yet exist.

The article's output is a research agenda, not a policy recommendation. The agenda has three parts.

**Build the datasets.** A public, source-graded dataset of military AI deployments by state, domain, and date is the single most valuable infrastructure the field could produce. Without it, the diffusion claim (7.1) and the survivability claim (7.3) remain untestable, and they will continue to circulate as findings by default.

**Restate the forecasts.** Authors making predictive claims should state scope conditions, signal conjectural status, and identify falsifiers. Editors and reviewers can require this. The cost is a paragraph. The benefit is that the field's forecasts become scoreable, which is the precondition for correcting them.

**Audit transmission.** The mechanism by which a declared forecast becomes a circulated finding operates in citing texts, not originating ones. A follow-up study should trace the ten most cited claims through their full citation networks and measure at each step which qualifiers survive. Table 4 of this article is a first estimate; a network study would be a second.

We began by saying that forecasting was the appropriate mode for this literature in its first decade. We end by saying that it remains appropriate, provided forecasts are declared as such. The field's task is not to stop forecasting. It is to make its forecasts the kind of thing that can be shown to be wrong.

## Appendix A. Codebook

### A.1 Unit of analysis

The unit is the claim: a single proposition asserted by the author as their own. A sentence may contain several claims; a claim may span several sentences. Coders record the location (section, paragraph) of each claim.

### A.2 Extraction rule

Extract all claims from abstract, introduction, and conclusion. Extract body claims marked as hypothesis, argument, contribution, or finding. Do not extract claims attributed to others without endorsement. Where endorsement is ambiguous, extract and flag.

### A.3 Claim type decision tree

1. Does the claim state what ought to be done? → Normative.
2. Does the claim define, classify, or propose a framework? → Conceptual.
3. Does the claim state a future state of affairs or a conditional future effect? → Predictive.
4. Does the claim state that one thing produces, increases, decreases, or conditions another, in the present or past? → Causal.
5. Otherwise → Descriptive.
6. If none of 1 to 5 can be applied, → Other, and record why in the notes.

Where 3 and 4 both apply to one proposition, the claim type is Predictive and the secondary claim type is Causal (Section 4.1). The claim occupies one record and is counted once. Coders do not create two records for one proposition.

### A.4 Warrant decision rules

- W1 requires that the author report data they collected or assembled and an analysis of it. Reanalysis of an existing dataset counts as W1.
- W2 requires that the author present original case material with sourcing. A case described from secondary literature only is W3.
- W3 requires a citation. Coders retrieve the cited source and sub-code its warrant. Where the cited source is not retrievable, sub-code as "unknown."
- W4 requires an explicit comparison to a prior technology or episode presented as grounds. Passing mention is not W4.
- W5 requires stated premises and an inferential step. Assertion of a mechanism without premises is W8.
- W6 requires citation of a person or institution's assertion without underlying data.
- W7 requires explicit marking: "we speculate," "hypothetically," "a plausible scenario," or equivalent.
- W8 is residual.

### A.5 Fit matrix

Rows are claim types. Columns are primary warrant types, with the four W3 sub-codes shown separately because they carry different fit. This is the complete table: every one of the 66 combinations the instrument can produce is given a value, and the same table applies under all four positioning codes. Two rules are then applied on top of it, and they are the only points at which fit departs from this lookup. The machine-readable copy is the `Fit_Matrix` sheet of the coding workbook, which expands these 66 rows across the four positioning codes to 264 keys.

| Claim type | W1 | W2 | W3→W1/W2 | W3→W3 | W3→W4–W8 | W3→unk. | W4 | W5 | W6 | W7 | W8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | A | A | A | P | P | P | P | I | P | P | I |
| Conceptual | A | A | A | A | A | A | A | A | P | A | P |
| Causal | A | A | A | P | P | P | P | P | I | P | I |
| Predictive | A | A | A | P | P | P | P | P | P | A | I |
| Normative | A | A | A | P | P | P | P | P | P | P | I |
| Other | P | P | P | P | P | P | P | P | P | P | P |

A = adequate; P = partial; I = inadequate. W3→unk. is a W3 claim whose cited source could not be retrieved.

**Rule 1, the declared-forecast test.** For a causal or predictive claim whose primary warrant is W7, the table value of A is granted only if all three declared-forecast indicators of Section 4.5 are present. With one or two indicators, or none, the fit is P. This applies under every positioning code. A conjecture that declares itself fully is adequately warranted as a conjecture; one that does not is not.

**Rule 2, the developed-analogy test.** For a predictive claim whose primary warrant is W4, in an article coded P2, P3, or P4, the table value of P is raised to A if the analogy is developed over at least one full section rather than invoked in passing. This is the one point at which positioning changes a fit value, and it is recorded in a dedicated `w4_developed` field so that the judgment is data rather than coder discretion. Under P1 the value stays P regardless.

**Normative claims** are assigned fit on the warrant offered for their empirical premise, using the Normative row above. The normative inference itself is not assessed. An earlier draft of this codebook instead redirected the coder to the row for the premise's own claim type; that produced two different answers for the same claim and was removed.

**P4 causal claims** are coded for fit on the same table and additionally flagged, since P4 authors often do not intend causal statements as tested propositions. They are reported in a separate table in Section 6.4 and are not excluded from the main distribution.

### A.6 Declared-forecast indicators

Scope conditions: any explicit statement of the conditions under which the claim holds. "In states with X" counts. "In general" does not.

Conjectural status: any explicit modal or structural marker. Placement in a section titled "Hypotheses" or "Scenarios" counts.

Falsifier: any statement, however brief, of what would count against the claim. "If X does not occur, the argument would be weakened" counts. Constructing a contrasting scenario does not count on its own: setting out what happens under condition A and under condition B states the claim's range, not the observation that would defeat it. Where an author does both, only the latter is recorded.

Originating text: for a claim whose primary warrant is W3, the coder opens the cited source, locates the proposition as the original author stated it, and records the same three indicators for that statement. Code the proposition as the cited author advanced it, not as the citing author reports it. Where the source cannot be retrieved, or the proposition cannot be located within it, record "unknown" for all three and note which of the two applies.

Reliability requires that both coders assign the same claim identifier to the same segment of text. Because coders extract claims independently before coding them, a reconciliation pass assigns shared identifiers to the segments both coders identified, and that pass precedes variable coding. Claims identified by only one coder are recorded in the unitisation statistic and are excluded from the variable α, which is computed on the reconciled set.
